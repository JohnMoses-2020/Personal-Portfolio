## Overview

This case study collects the recurring themes from working on large-scale
Kubernetes infrastructure: keeping availability high while the platform grows,
treating capacity as a first-class engineering problem, and making deployments
boring through controlled, observable rollouts.

## Problem statement

As a platform grows, three pressures tend to arrive together:

- **Availability expectations rise** faster than the team's ability to review
  every change by hand.
- **Capacity becomes a moving target** — demand shifts across regions and
  workloads, and headroom that looked generous last quarter is suddenly tight.
- **Deployment risk compounds**: more clusters, more tenants, and more moving
  parts mean a bad rollout can affect far more than it used to.

The engineering challenge is to build processes and tooling that let the
platform scale without scaling the on-call burden at the same rate.

## Constraints

- Changes must be safe to roll out across many clusters without manual
  babysitting.
- Capacity decisions need to be explainable: why a request was approved,
  deferred, or rejected.
- Observability has to work at fleet level, not just per cluster.
- Everything must coexist with existing production workloads — no
  "big-bang" migrations.

## Architecture and approach

At a high level, the approach treats the fleet as a set of layers:

<Diagram title="Fleet-level layering (conceptual)">

```text
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Policy & intent        what should run where, and why   │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Capacity management    headroom, validation, forecasts  │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Rollout orchestration  staged, observable, reversible   │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Clusters               the actual Kubernetes control    │
│                         planes and node pools            │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

</Diagram>

Each layer owns a narrow responsibility and exposes signals upward, so a
rollout can be paused by a capacity signal, and a capacity decision can be
traced back to policy.

## Technical decisions and tradeoffs

### Staged rollouts over fast rollouts

Rolling out to a small slice first costs time, but it converts unknown risk
into observed behavior. The tradeoff is accepted deliberately: a slower,
boring rollout is cheaper than a fast incident.

### Disruption budgets as a contract

Pod disruption budgets make availability expectations explicit to the
scheduler and to humans. A representative (non-proprietary) example:

```yaml title="pdb.yaml" showLineNumbers
apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: api-gateway
spec:
  minAvailable: 80%
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: api-gateway
```

### Capacity validation before scheduling

Checking headroom before accepting new work trades a little latency in the
provisioning path for far fewer "scheduled but pending" surprises later.

## Lessons learned

- Make the safe path the easy path. If a controlled rollout takes more effort
  than a manual one, people will route around it.
- Capacity is a product. Give it an owner, an interface, and a feedback loop.
- Fleet-level observability pays for itself the first time a problem spans
  more than one cluster.
